Chapter 01

Two Kinds of Questions

Machine learning answers questions about data. Almost every question is one of two kinds. In regression the answer is a number: how much fuel will a 1500 kg car use? In classification the answer is a category: is this penguin an Adelie or a Gentoo?

Regression Classification
Question How much fuel will this car use? Which penguin is this?
Input Weight: 1500 kg Flipper: 215 mm
Answer A number (L / 100 km) A category (Adelie or Gentoo)

The way a machine learns is always the same. We have data $Z$. A learning algorithm looks at it and produces a model $f$. Later, a new input arrives, and the model gives a predicted output. Today we pick the simplest family of models there is: a straight line.

Intuition Check: Think of a model as a vending machine. You put in an input, a prediction drops out. Learning means tuning the machine until the predictions are good.
Chapter 02

A Model with One Knob

Start tiny. We know three examples: $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(3, 3)$. Each is one dot: an input $x$ and its true answer $y$. A new input arrives, $x = 4$. What answer should we expect? To say anything, we need a model.

Our machine is the simplest possible: it multiplies the input by a number $\beta$.

$$\hat{y} = f_\beta(x) = \beta x$$

Here $\hat{y}$ ("y-hat") is the prediction: what the model says the answer is. The number $\beta$ is a knob we can turn. Try a guess, $\beta = 1.5$. Now the same machine gives different predictions for different inputs:

Input $x$ Machine Prediction $\hat{y}$ True $y$
1 × 1.5 1.5 1
2 × 1.5 3 2
3 × 1.5 4.5 3

Geometrically, the model is a line through the origin, and $\beta$ is its slope. To predict, pick $x$, go up to the line, and read the height. For $x = 2$ the line gives $\hat{y} = 3$, but reality is $y = 2$. The gap, $3 - 2 = 1$, is the error. A good $\beta$ makes the errors small. But how do we pick it?

Chapter 03

Loss: How Wrong Is the Line?

For every example the error is the gap between prediction and reality, $y_i - \beta x_i$. We want one number that says how wrong the whole model is. Square each error (so that misses above and below do not cancel) and take the average. This is the mean squared error (MSE), our loss function:

$$L(\beta; Z) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \beta x_i \right)^2$$

It is computationally convenient and works well in practice. Now try different slopes on our three dots and compute the loss for each:

Slope $\beta$ Loss $L(\beta; Z)$ Verdict
0.25 2.6 line too flat
0.5 1.2 better
1 0 the line passes through every dot
1.5 1.2 too steep, as bad as 0.5
2 4.7 much too steep

Plot the loss against $\beta$ and it forms a bowl. The bottom of the bowl is the best line. Drag the slider below and watch the line, the red errors and the dot on the bowl move together.

Intuition Check: A bowl has exactly one lowest point, and from any spot on its wall you can tell which way is down. That is the whole trick behind the next chapter.
Why is the loss for our three dots exactly a bowl?

On our dots $y_i = x_i$, so each error is $y_i - \beta x_i = (1 - \beta) x_i$. Squaring and averaging gives:

$$L(\beta) = \frac{(1-\beta)^2}{3}\,(1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2) = \frac{14}{3}\,(1-\beta)^2$$

A parabola with its bottom at $\beta = 1$. Check the table: $\beta = 0.25$ gives $\tfrac{14}{3} \cdot 0.5625 \approx 2.6$ and $\beta = 2$ gives $\tfrac{14}{3} \approx 4.7$.

Optional: why squared error? The likelihood view

Suppose reality is the line plus random noise, $y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i$, where the noise is Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2$. The likelihood of the data is the product of the probabilities of every $y_i$. Take the logarithm and flip the sign (the negative log-likelihood):

$$-\log p(y \mid X, \beta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \beta x_i \right)^2 + \text{const}$$

Maximizing the likelihood is therefore the same as minimizing the squared errors. MSE is not an arbitrary choice: it is what "the best $\beta$ for the data" means when the noise is Gaussian.

Chapter 04

Gradient Descent: Walking Downhill

We could try every $\beta$ by hand, but a computer can do better: stand on the bowl, feel which way is downhill, and take a step. Say we stand at $\beta = 0.25$. Nudge it a little, to $0.26$, and the loss drops from $2.63$ to $2.56$. The slope is about $-0.07 / 0.01 = -7.0$. A negative slope means the loss falls as $\beta$ grows, so the bottom is to the right.

The rule is: walk against the slope. How far? The step size $\eta$ (also called the learning rate) decides.

$$\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta \cdot \text{slope}, \qquad \text{slope} = \frac{dL}{d\beta} = -\frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \left( y_i - \beta x_i \right)$$

Take $\eta = 0.1$ and start at $\beta = 0.25$. Each step uses the slope at the current spot:

Step Slope at $\beta$ Update New $\beta$
1 $-7.0$ $0.25 - 0.1 \times (-7.0)$ 0.95
2 $-0.47$ $0.95 - 0.1 \times (-0.47)$ 0.997
3 $\approx -0.03$ tiny step 1.000

Notice how the steps shrink near the bottom: the ground gets flatter, so the slope, and therefore the step, gets smaller. The walk is $0.250 \to 0.950 \to 0.997 \to 1.000$.

Paste this into a new Google Colab notebook 
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1., 2., 3.])
y = np.array([1., 2., 3.])
beta, eta = 0.25, 0.1

for step in range(1, 4):
    slope = -2 * np.mean(x * (y - beta * x))
    beta = beta - eta * slope
    print(step, round(slope, 2), round(beta, 3))

# 1 -7.0 0.95
# 2 -0.47 0.997
# 3 -0.03 1.0

The step size matters

Too small a step is slow. Too big a step jumps over the bottom and bounces away. Choosing $\eta$ well is part of the craft. Try it yourself: set the step size, then press Step and watch $\beta$ and the loss.

Common Pitfall: For our three dots every step multiplies the distance to the bottom by $1 - \tfrac{28}{3}\eta$. If $\eta > 0.21$ or so, this factor is below $-1$, so each step lands farther from $\beta = 1$ than the last one, and the loss grows instead of falling. This is what $\eta = 0.3$ does on the slide.
$$\underbrace{\text{Model}}_{\hat{y} = \beta x} \;\to\; \underbrace{\text{Loss}}_{\text{MSE}} \;\to\; \underbrace{\text{Optimize}}_{\text{gradient descent}}$$

This is the recipe of machine learning: pick a model, measure how wrong it is with a loss, and find the knobs with the smallest loss. Linear regression, logistic regression and neural networks all follow it.

Chapter 05

Real Data: Cars and Two Knobs

Three dots were a warm-up. Now real data: 398 cars from the 1970s and 80s. Each dot is one car, with its weight in tonnes and its fuel use in litres per 100 km. Heavier cars burn more fuel, so a line should capture the trend. The question: a car weighs 1.5 t, how much fuel will it use?

A line through the origin is too strict for real cars. A line needs two numbers: where it starts and how steep it is. The slope $\beta_1$ is the steepness, the intercept $\beta_0$ is the height at weight $0$. Every pair $(\beta_0, \beta_1)$ is a different line.

$$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$$

This is linear regression with one feature: the input goes in, is multiplied by $\beta_1$, then $\beta_0$ is added. Try a guess, $\beta_0 = 0$ and $\beta_1 = 6$. A $1.5$ t car gets $\hat{y} = 0 + 6 \times 1.5 = 9$ litres per 100 km. Play with both knobs below.

The loss, again

Same recipe as before. For every car the error is prediction minus reality, and the loss is the average of the squared errors over all cars. Our guess $(0, 6)$ has $L = 14.4$. With both knobs at $0$ the line is flat and the loss is $140.9$.

From one knob to two

With two knobs the bowl becomes a surface. Seen from above it is a map of rings: every ring is a set of lines with the same loss. Each knob feels its own slope and steps against it. All the slopes together are called the gradient.

$$\beta_0 \leftarrow \beta_0 - \eta \cdot \text{slope}_0, \qquad \beta_1 \leftarrow \beta_1 - \eta \cdot \text{slope}_1$$

Starting from $(0, 0)$ the walk looks like this. The line tilts up, the loss drops fast, then the steps shrink because the ground flattens near the bottom.

Intuition Check: Between step 5 and step 100 the loss only goes from $9.3$ to $3.9$, but $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ keep changing. The path bends along a narrow valley: the bowl is long and thin, so the walk is slow near the end. Remember this, it is the reason for scaling in the next chapter.

When do we stop?

We cannot walk forever. Pick a small number $\varepsilon$, the threshold, and stop when one step lowers the loss by less than $\varepsilon$. The three choices below were measured on the cars.

Threshold $\varepsilon$ Stops at step Loss Verdict
0.1105.0too big: still far from 3.3

Common Pitfall: A threshold alone is not safe. In practice also set a maximum number of steps, just in case the loss never settles.

The best line for the cars is $\hat{y} = -0.9 + 9.0\,x$. The loss fell from $14.4$ to $3.3$, and a $1.5$ t car is predicted to use about $-0.9 + 9.0 \times 1.5 \approx 12.6$ litres per 100 km.

Many Features: Penguins

Cars had one feature. Now each penguin has four: flipper length, bill length, bill depth and sex ($0$ = female, $1$ = male). The goal is to predict body mass. There are 333 penguins from Palmer Station, Antarctica. More features means more knobs, but the recipe stays: model, loss, optimize.

Model: every feature gets a knob

Multiply each input by its own knob, add everything up, then add $\beta_0$. One feature gives $2$ knobs (a line). Four features give $5$ knobs. Nothing else changes.

$$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_3 + \beta_4 x_4$$

Loss: the same one

Start with all knobs at $0$, so every prediction is $0$ kg. The error of each penguin is just minus its real mass, and the squared errors are large.

Penguin Real mass $y$ Prediction $\hat{y}$ Error $\hat{y} - y$ Error²

#1513.730.00−3.7213.8

The average over all $333$ penguins is $L = 18.3$. Huge, because the line sits at $0$. The walk downhill will fix that.

Optimize: first, the same scale

Features live on very different scales: flipper is about $200$, bill depth about $17$, and sex is $0$ or $1$. A knob on a big feature moves the loss much more than a knob on a small one, so the bowl becomes a long narrow valley, exactly like the cars. The fix is to rescale each feature: subtract its average and divide by its spread.

$$x_{\text{new}} = \frac{x - \text{average}}{\text{spread}}$$

After rescaling every feature sits roughly between $-3$ and $3$. Equal scales make the bowl round, and the walk goes almost straight to the center.

Do more features help?

Yes, a little. Flipper length alone reaches a loss of $0.154$, all four features reach $0.114$. The knobs also tell a story: $+1$ mm of flipper is about $+39$ g, a male penguin is about $+541$ g heavier, and bill length is close to $0$, so it adds almost nothing once the other features are known.

Chapter 07

Logistic Regression: Yes or No

Now the answer is a category, not a number: which penguin is this, an Adelie or a Gentoo? We code it as a number, Adelie $= 0$ and Gentoo $= 1$, and use the flipper length as the input. Gentoo flippers are longer, but the two groups overlap a bit.

Why not just a straight line?

Try linear regression on the $0/1$ labels. The line goes below $0$ and above $1$, which makes no sense for a yes/no answer. We want an output that always stays between $0$ and $1$: a probability.

Squash the line: the sigmoid

The sigmoid squeezes any number $z$ into the range $0$ to $1$. A big negative number goes close to $0$, zero goes to exactly $0.5$, and a big positive number goes close to $1$.

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

The logistic model

Take the linear machine from before and add one new step at the end: the sigmoid. The output $\hat{p}$ is now the probability that the penguin is a Gentoo.

$$\hat{p} = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)$$

After training, the curve climbs from $0$ to $1$ right where the two groups meet. The rule is simple: $\hat{p} > 0.5$ means Gentoo, otherwise Adelie. For a flipper of $215$ mm the model gives $\hat{p} > 0.99$, so Gentoo. For $205$ mm it gives $\hat{p} \approx 0.44$, so we are unsure. The boundary sits at about $205$ mm.

How wrong is a probability?

If the penguin really is a Gentoo ($y = 1$), we want $\hat{p}$ close to $1$. The loss should punish a confident mistake much harder than a small one. The answer is the log loss, also called cross-entropy: minus the logarithm of the probability given to the true class.

$$\ell = \begin{cases} -\ln(\hat{p}) & \text{if } y = 1 \ (\text{Gentoo}) \\ -\ln(1 - \hat{p}) & \text{if } y = 0 \ (\text{Adelie}) \end{cases}$$
True class Model says Loss
Gentoo ($y=1$)$\hat{p} = 0.99$$-\ln(0.99) = 0.01$
Gentoo ($y=1$)$\hat{p} = 0.50$$-\ln(0.50) = 0.69$
Gentoo ($y=1$)$\hat{p} = 0.05$$-\ln(0.05) = 3.0$
Adelie ($y=0$)$\hat{p} = 0.05$ for Gentoo$-\ln(0.95) = 0.05$

The training loss is the average of this number over all penguins, just like the average of squared errors in linear regression.

Common Pitfall: Log loss is not defined at exactly $\hat{p} = 0$ or $1$ for the wrong class, because $-\ln(0)$ is infinite. This is why the slider above stops at $0.01$ and $0.99$.

Chapter 08

One Neuron, Then a Network

A neuron adds up its inputs, each multiplied by a weight, and then applies the sigmoid. That is exactly the logistic model we just built, with more inputs allowed. One neuron is logistic regression.

Connect many neurons in layers, so that the outputs of one layer become the inputs of the next, and you get a neural network. The loss is the same, gradient descent is the same, there are just more knobs to turn.

Why this matters

Everything in this lesson is how modern AI learns. Bigger models, same idea: a model, a loss, and a walk downhill. Language models read and write text, vision models recognize faces, cars and tumors in scans, speech models power voice assistants and translation, and game and robot agents learn to move and to play.

Intuition Check: Our line for the cars has $2$ parameters. A modern neural network has billions. The knobs multiply, the recipe does not change.

What We Learned

Three ideas carry from a tiny line all the way to neural networks.

1 · Model
A simple machine that turns an input into a prediction.
2 · Loss
One number that says how wrong the model is.
3 · Optimize
Walk downhill on the loss with gradient descent.

Glossary & Terminology

Regression / classification
Two kinds of questions: the answer is a number, or the answer is a category.
Model, $\hat{y}$
A machine that turns an input into a prediction. $\hat{y}$ (“y-hat”) is the prediction.
Knob, parameter, $\beta$
A number inside the model that training adjusts. $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta_1$ the slope.
Error
The gap between prediction and reality for one example.
Loss, MSE
One number for how wrong the model is. Mean squared error is the average of the squared errors.
Gradient descent
Walk downhill on the loss: $\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta \cdot \text{slope}$.
Step size, learning rate, $\eta$
How far one step goes. Too small is slow, too big overshoots.
Gradient
All the slopes of the loss, one for each knob, taken together.
Threshold, $\varepsilon$
Stop when one step lowers the loss by less than $\varepsilon$.
Rescaling
Subtract the average and divide by the spread, so all features share one scale.
Sigmoid, $\sigma$
A function that squeezes any number into $(0, 1)$.
Log loss, cross-entropy
Minus the log of the probability given to the true class. The loss of logistic regression.
Neuron, neural network
A weighted sum plus a sigmoid. A network is many neurons in layers.
